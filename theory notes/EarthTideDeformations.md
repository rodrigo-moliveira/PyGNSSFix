# Earth Tides and Reference Frame Conventions

## 1. Introduction

The Earth's crust is continuously deformed by gravitational and rotational effects. These deformations cause the physical position of a GNSS reference station to vary with time, even when the monument itself is stable with respect to the surrounding crust.

For high-precision GNSS applications, these effects must be accounted for in the observation model. Otherwise, the estimated station coordinates will contain apparent variations associated with geophysical effects rather than only the actual motion of the station.

The main displacement models considered in the IERS Conventions are:

- **Solid Earth tides:** deformation caused primarily by the gravitational attraction of the Moon and the Sun.
- **Ocean tidal loading:** deformation caused by the redistribution of ocean masses due to ocean tides.
- **Atmospheric loading:** deformation caused by variations in atmospheric pressure.
- **Pole tide:** deformation caused by variations in the Earth's rotation axis with respect to the crust.
- **Ocean pole tide:** deformation caused by the redistribution of ocean masses associated with polar motion.

An important aspect of these models is the treatment of the permanent tidal deformation. Depending on how this contribution is handled, the resulting station coordinates belong to different tide systems.

The distinction is particularly important when comparing GNSS-derived coordinates with coordinates from a terrestrial reference frame such as ITRF2020.

---

## 2. Permanent and periodic tidal deformation

The tidal potential generated by external bodies, primarily the Moon and the Sun, can be decomposed into time-independent and time-dependent components.

Similarly, the deformation induced in the Earth's crust contains both permanent and periodic contributions.

The instantaneous position of a reference marker can therefore be expressed conceptually as:

\[
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{inst}}(t) = \mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{reference}} + \Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}
+ \Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

where:

- \(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{reference}}\) is the reference position of the station.
- \(\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}\) is the time-independent deformation associated with the permanent tidal potential.
- \(\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)\) represents the time-dependent tidal deformation.

The periodic component contains contributions at different frequencies, including diurnal, semidiurnal and long-period variations.

The permanent component, on the other hand, does not vary with time. It represents the equilibrium deformation associated with the zero-frequency component of the tidal potential.

This distinction leads to the definition of different permanent tide conventions.

---

## 3. Permanent tide conventions

Three conventions are commonly encountered in geodesy:

1. Tide-free.
2. Mean-tide.
3. Zero-tide.

Although the terminology is similar, an important distinction exists between the conventions applied to station coordinates (the Earth's crust) and those applied to the geopotential.

### 3.1 Tide-free convention

In the tide-free convention, both the permanent and periodic tidal deformations are removed from the station coordinates.

Conceptually:

\[
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{TF}} =
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
-
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}
-
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

The resulting coordinates represent an idealized crust without tidal deformation.

However, a truly tide-free crust is not physically observable because the Moon and Sun are always present.

Furthermore, the conventional tide-free system used by IERS is not strictly equivalent to a hypothetical Earth without tidal perturbations. The conventional models use nominal Love and Shida numbers rather than the secular (fluid-limit) values that would characterize the response to the permanent tide over geological timescales.

Consequently, the term **conventional tide-free** is used to describe the reference system obtained using the adopted IERS tidal models.

### 3.2 Mean-tide convention

In the mean-tide convention, only the periodic tidal deformation is removed.

The permanent deformation remains part of the station coordinates:

\[
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{MT}} =
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
-
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{MT}} =
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{TF}}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}
}
\]

Mean-tide coordinates represent the mean shape of the crust, including the permanent deformation caused by the tidal potential.

### 3.3 Zero-tide convention

For station coordinates, the zero-tide convention is synonymous with the mean-tide convention.

However, this equivalence does not hold for the geopotential.

For the geopotential:

- **Mean-tide:** includes both the permanent tidal potential and the associated permanent deformation contribution.
- **Zero-tide:** removes the permanent direct tidal potential but retains the deformation-related contribution.
- **Tide-free:** removes both permanent contributions.

This distinction is important when working with gravity field models and geopotential coefficients, particularly the degree-2 zonal coefficient \(C_{20}\).

Therefore, the meaning of zero-tide must always be interpreted in the context of the quantity being considered.

### 3.4 Summary of the conventions

| Convention | Permanent crustal deformation | Periodic crustal deformation |
|---|---|---|
| Tide-free | Removed | Removed |
| Mean-tide | Retained | Removed |
| Zero-tide (crust) | Retained | Removed |
| Instantaneous | Retained | Retained |

For station coordinates, zero-tide and mean-tide are equivalent.

## 4. Transformations between tide systems

The transformations between tide systems are based on the permanent deformation associated with the degree-2 zonal component of the tide-generating potential.

The relationship between conventional tide-free and mean-tide coordinates is:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{MT}} =
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{TF}}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}
}
\]

Conversely:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{TF}} =
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{MT}}
-
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}
}
\]

The permanent deformation is a function of the station latitude and contains both radial and horizontal components.

### 4.1 Permanent deformation according to IERS 2010

According to IERS Conventions 2010, Section 7.1.1.2, the permanent deformation can be expressed as:

Radial component:

\[
\Delta r =
\left[
-0.1206 + 0.0001P_2(\sin\varphi)
\right]
P_2(\sin\varphi)
\]

Horizontal component (northwards):

\[
\Delta n =
\left[
-0.0252 - 0.0001P_2(\sin\varphi)
\right]
\sin(2\varphi)
\]

where:

\[
P_2(\sin\varphi) =
\frac{1}{2}\left(3\sin^2\varphi-1\right)
\]

and \(\varphi\) is the geocentric latitude.

The radial component is expressed in metres and is positive outwards. The horizontal component is expressed in metres and is positive northwards.

The corresponding ECEF displacement vector can be obtained by transforming the radial and northward components into the terrestrial reference frame.

The radial component of the transformation amounts approximately to:

- \(-12\) cm at the poles.
- \(+6\) cm at the equator.

The horizontal component is smaller but must also be considered when performing precise coordinate transformations.

These equations describe the vector that must be **added to conventional tide-free coordinates to obtain mean-tide coordinates**.

### 4.2 Important interpretation

The permanent deformation equations are not an additional periodic correction.

They represent a constant, latitude-dependent transformation between coordinate conventions.

Therefore, they should not be confused with the time-dependent solid Earth tide corrections applied at each GNSS observation epoch.

## 5. IERS conventions for solid Earth tides

The IERS Conventions 2010, Chapter 7, describe the conventional models for displacement of reference markers on the Earth's crust.

Section 7.1.1 describes the effects of solid Earth tides.

The model is based on the deformation induced by the tidal potential and uses Love and Shida numbers to describe the elastic response of the Earth.

The model includes several contributions.

### 5.1 Time-domain corrections

The first step computes the main tidal displacement contributions in the time domain.

These contributions are associated with the gravitational attraction of the Moon and the Sun.

The deformation includes radial and horizontal components and depends on:

- Station position.
- Positions of the Moon and Sun.
- Geocentric distance to the disturbing bodies.
- Love and Shida numbers.
- Earth rotation and station geometry.

### 5.2 Frequency-dependent corrections

The second step introduces frequency-dependent corrections to the nominal Love and Shida numbers.

The Earth's response is not identical at all tidal frequencies.

In particular, the frequency dependence is important in the diurnal band due to the nearly diurnal free wobble (NDFW) resonance associated with the free core nutation.

These corrections improve the accuracy of the predicted tidal displacement.

### 5.3 Permanent deformation

The complete solid Earth tide displacement model contains a time-independent component.

Consequently, when the full model is applied to GNSS observations, the estimated coordinates correspond to **conventional tide-free coordinates**.

This is explicitly stated in IERS Conventions 2010, Section 7.1.1.2.

The same section provides the permanent deformation equations described in Section 4, allowing users to transform conventional tide-free coordinates into mean-tide coordinates.

An important distinction is that the permanent deformation is included in the complete displacement model. It is not automatically removed by the standard solid Earth tide calculation.

## 6. Implications for GNSS positioning

In a high-precision GNSS observation model, the station coordinates should represent a well-defined reference point, normally the station monument marker.

The physical position of the monument at an observation epoch can be expressed as:

\[
\mathbf{r}_{M}(t) =
\mathbf{r}_{M_0}(t)
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{solid}}(t)
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t)
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{pole}}(t)
\]

where:

- \(\mathbf{r}_{M_0}(t)\) represents the conventional reference position of the monument.
- \(\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{solid}}(t)\) represents solid Earth tide displacement.
- \(\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t)\) represents ocean tidal loading displacement.
- \(\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{pole}}(t)\) represents pole tide displacement.

The antenna reference point (ARP) and antenna phase centre (APC) must then be constructed from the monument position using the station eccentricity and antenna calibration.

Conceptually:

\[
\mathbf{r}_{ARP}(t) =
\mathbf{r}_{M}(t)
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{ARP}
\]

\[
\mathbf{r}_{APC}(t) =
\mathbf{r}_{ARP}(t)
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{PCO}
\]

The receiver PCV correction is applied separately in the observation model.

Therefore, if the complete solid Earth tide model is applied at each observation epoch, including its permanent component, and the monument-to-ARP eccentricity and receiver antenna calibration are correctly handled, the estimated station coordinates correspond to conventional tide-free monument coordinates.

### 6.1 Applying the displacement through the LOS

For a linearized range model, the effect of a receiver displacement can be projected onto the satellite-receiver line of sight.

Let the LOS unit vector point from the receiver towards the satellite:

\[
\mathbf{e}_{LOS} =
\frac{\mathbf{r}_s-\mathbf{r}_r}
{\|\mathbf{r}_s-\mathbf{r}_r\|}
\]

For a small receiver displacement \(\Delta\mathbf{r}_r\), the corresponding geometric range variation is:

\[
\Delta\rho =
-\mathbf{e}_{LOS}^{T}\Delta\mathbf{r}_r
\]

Thus, the predicted pseudorange can be expressed conceptually as:

\[
P_{\mathrm{pred}} =
\rho_{\mathrm{geom}}(\mathbf{r}_{M_0})
-
\mathbf{e}_{LOS}^{T}
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{receiver}}
+
\mathrm{PCV}
+
\mathrm{other\ corrections}
\]

where:

\[
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{receiver}} =
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{solid}}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{ocean}}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{pole}}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{ARP}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{PCO}
\]

The signs must be adapted to the specific LOS and observation-model conventions.

## 7. What happens if the permanent deformation is removed?

An important implementation detail is whether the permanent component is retained in the solid Earth tide displacement or explicitly removed.

Let the complete IERS solid Earth tide displacement be:

\[
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{solid}}(t) =
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

There are two possible implementations.

### Case A: Permanent deformation is retained

The complete displacement is applied:

\[
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{applied}}(t) =
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{permanent}}
+
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

The estimated reference coordinates are:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{estimated}} =
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{conventional\ tide-free}}
}
\]

This is consistent with ITRF conventional tide-free coordinates.

### Case B: Permanent deformation is removed

Only the periodic displacement is applied:

\[
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{applied}}(t) =
\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

The permanent deformation is then absorbed into the estimated reference coordinates:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{estimated}} =
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{mean-tide}}
}
\]

This is because the periodic component is removed from the instantaneous station position, but the permanent component remains.

Consequently, removing the permanent component from the displacement correction does not produce tide-free coordinates. It produces mean-tide coordinates.

### 7.1 Practical comparison

| Solid Earth tide treatment | Estimated coordinate convention |
|---|---|
| Full displacement, including permanent component | Conventional tide-free |
| Periodic displacement only | Mean-tide |
| No solid Earth tide correction | Contains periodic and permanent tidal effects, subject to the estimation process |

The last case is not a well-defined tide convention because the estimated coordinates may absorb the tidal effects according to the observation sampling, weighting and parameterization.

## 8. ITRF2020 and the tide convention

The International Terrestrial Reference Frame (ITRF) uses the conventional tide-free convention for station coordinates.

Therefore, ITRF2020 station coordinates are consistent with the conventional tide-free treatment of solid Earth tides.

This means that daily PPP solutions intended for direct comparison with ITRF2020 should use:

- The complete IERS solid Earth tide displacement model, including the permanent component.
- Consistent ocean tidal loading corrections.
- Consistent pole tide corrections.
- Appropriate station eccentricities and antenna calibrations.

The ITRF reference coordinates and velocities should also be propagated to the epoch of the daily PPP solution.

For a station without discontinuities or additional deformation models:

\[
\mathbf{r}_{ITRF}(t) =
\mathbf{r}_{ITRF}(t_{\mathrm{ref}})
+
\mathbf{v}_{ITRF}(t-t_{\mathrm{ref}})
\]

More complete propagation may need to account for documented discontinuities, post-seismic deformation and other station-specific models.

When comparing daily PPP solutions with ITRF2020, both coordinate sets should refer to the same physical point (the monument marker), reference frame, epoch and permanent tide convention.

---

## 9. Recommended convention for a PPP processor

For a PPP processor designed to produce daily station coordinates suitable for comparison with ITRF2020, the recommended treatment is:

| Model or parameter | Recommended treatment |
|---|---|
| Solid Earth tides | Full IERS model, including permanent deformation |
| Ocean tidal loading | Time-dependent displacement model |
| Pole tide | IERS pole tide model |
| Ocean pole tide | Include when required by the adopted IERS model |
| Monument-to-ARP eccentricity | Apply using station metadata |
| Receiver PCO | Apply using ANTEX |
| Receiver PCV | Apply using ANTEX |
| Estimated station coordinates | Conventional tide-free monument coordinates |
| Reference coordinates | ITRF2020 conventional tide-free coordinates |

It is important not to apply the permanent solid Earth tide transformation indiscriminately to ocean loading or pole tide corrections. These are separate physical displacement models.

Furthermore, seasonal hydrological loading, non-tidal atmospheric loading and other geophysical effects may still be present in the resulting coordinate time series unless explicitly modelled.

## 11. Main conclusions

- The solid Earth tide displacement contains both permanent and periodic contributions.
- Applying the complete IERS solid Earth tide model, including the permanent component, results in conventional tide-free estimated station coordinates.
- Removing the permanent component from the displacement model results in mean-tide estimated station coordinates.
- For station coordinates, zero-tide and mean-tide are equivalent.
- For the geopotential, zero-tide and mean-tide are different conventions.
- ITRF2020 uses conventional tide-free station coordinates.
- Daily PPP solutions intended for comparison with ITRF2020 should retain the permanent deformation in the solid Earth tide correction.
- The monument-to-ARP eccentricity, receiver PCO and PCV must be handled separately and consistently to ensure that the estimated coordinates refer to the monument marker.

12. Complete IERS solid Earth tide displacement model

According to IERS Conventions 2010, Chapter 7, Section 7.1.1, the conventional solid Earth tide displacement model consists of two steps:

Step 1: Time-domain corrections

The first step computes the main solid Earth tide displacement contributions in the time domain.

These contributions are caused primarily by the gravitational attraction of the Moon and the Sun.

The resulting displacement accounts for the radial and horizontal deformation of the Earth's crust and includes the permanent component associated with the tidal potential.

Step 2: Frequency-dependent corrections

The second step introduces frequency-dependent corrections to the nominal Love and Shida numbers.

These corrections account for the fact that the Earth's response to tidal forcing varies with frequency.

In particular, the diurnal frequency band requires special treatment due to the nearly diurnal free wobble (NDFW) resonance associated with the Earth's free core nutation.

The frequency-dependent corrections are added to the results of Step 1.

Consequently, the complete solid Earth tide displacement is:

[
\boxed{
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{solid}}(t) =
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{step1}}(t)
+
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{step2}}(t)
}
]

This displacement contains both:

The permanent (time-independent) deformation.

The periodic (time-dependent) deformation.

12.1 Coordinate convention resulting from the complete model

IERS Conventions 2010, Section 7.1.1.2, explains that the complete solid Earth tide model contains the permanent deformation.

When the complete model is applied to GNSS observations, the estimated station coordinates correspond to conventional tide-free coordinates.

This is the coordinate convention adopted by ITRF.

The relationship between conventional tide-free and mean-tide coordinates is:

[
\boxed{
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{MT}} =
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{TF}}
+
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{permanent}}
}
]

Therefore, to obtain mean-tide coordinates from conventional tide-free coordinates, the permanent deformation must be added back to the coordinates.

Importantly, this transformation is not part of the standard Step 1 + Step 2 solid Earth tide displacement calculation. It is a separate coordinate-system transformation.

12.2 Implementation in a GNSS processor

For a PPP processor intended to produce station coordinates consistent with ITRF2020, the displacement model should retain the permanent deformation.

The appropriate implementation is:

# IERS Conventions 2010, Chapter 7
# Steps 1 and 2: Complete solid Earth tide displacement.
# Permanent deformation is retained.

disp_ecef = disp_step1 + disp_step2

The resulting displacement is applied at each observation epoch through the receiver position correction in the GNSS observation model.

The following implementation should therefore be avoided when conventional tide-free coordinates are required:

disp_ecef = disp_step1 + disp_step2 - disp_step3

provided that disp_step3 represents the permanent deformation.

Subtracting the permanent component leaves only the periodic displacement and results in estimated mean-tide coordinates.


### 1. Full model: Step 1 + Step 2

Your observation model is:

\[
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
=
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{est}}
+
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{perm}}
+
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

Rearranging:

\[
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{est}}
=
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
-
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{perm}}
-
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\mathbf r_{\mathrm{est}}=\mathbf r_{\mathrm{TF}}}
\]

Your estimated position is tide-free.

### 2. Periodic-only model: Step 1 + Step 2 − Step 3

Your observation model becomes:

\[
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
=
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{est}}
+
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

Rearranging:

\[
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{est}}
=
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
-
\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{periodic}}(t)
\]

Since the permanent deformation is no longer modelled, it remains in the estimated reference position:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{est}}
=
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{TF}}+\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{perm}}
=
\mathbf r_{\mathrm{MT}}
}
\]

Your estimated position is mean-tide.
